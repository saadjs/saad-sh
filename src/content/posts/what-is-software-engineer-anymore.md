---
title: "What Does It Mean to Be a Software Engineer Anymore?"
description: "Thinking out loud about what changes for software engineers when building/writing code is easy."
date: "2026-10-12"
tags: ["AI"]
published: true
---

It has never been this easy to build software and its going to keep getting easier. I find that exciting and terrifying at the same time and I've been thinking about what it means to be a “software engineer”.

The _**exciting**_ part is that building software is no longer the hard part. A side project can be built in 1-2 prompts. With the right setup I don't even need a 💻. I can build software from my 📱 and bring it into existence. I can also take on work outside my own area. Most of the repetitive work is gone, and trying a new language or tool takes an afternoon.

The _**terrifying**_ part is that the job is changing and the uncertainty about what the future holds.

For a long time, being a software engineer could mean picking up a Jira ticket that product wrote, write the code, and moving on to the next one. This literally is one prompt now (or no prompt if the process of creating a ticket to PR is automated ‼️). I think engineers whose value comes primarily from completing assigned tickets are going to have a hard time.

But there's an important distinction between building software and building a product.

A small tool I build for myself isn't the same as a product used by thousands of people. I'm the only user of my side projects. A real product has users, a team, a business behind it, and the expectation that it will still work a year(s) from now. A few prompts aren't enough to guarantee that.

And I think there are two things that agents haven't really made easier.

_**First is knowing what to build.**_

when anyone can build anything, knowing what to becomes important, companies who have established themselves in the industry are at great advantage due to amount of data they have about their products/users.

and from the teams perspective, the lines between design, product and engineering are blurring everyday. As an engineer I now have to know who our users are, how they use what we built, what feedback they give, and what the business needs. None of that is written in a ticket.

_**Second would be knowing whether what we built is actually good.**_

Agents write code that runs. I still have to judge whether it is well designed, whether it fits the rest of the codebase, and whether it will hold up with ten times more users.

That judgment requires fundamentals. I can only ask for a better design if I understand what a good one looks like. And I can only push back on a bad one if I can recognize why it's bad.

I think this is what people mean when they talk about having _**“taste”**_.

There's also a part of this that makes me a little nostalgic.

Googling errors, reading Stack Overflow, spending hours or days reproducing a bug, stepping through code line by line, adding console logs everywhere, and getting code review feedback that forced me to rethink an entire implementation.

Those experiences shaped how I learned to engineer software. They were frustrating at the time, but they taught me a lot.

Now, so much of that can be replaced with something like: _“Hey, I'm seeing XYZ behavior when I try ABC. Can you reproduce it and fix it?”_
And most of the time, it just works!

I don't know whether that's entirely a good thing or a bad thing. I love that I can spend less time fighting tools and more time building things. But I also wonder what we're losing along the way.

The center of gravity of software engineering is moving from implementation to judgment, and that has consequences for both who thrives in this industry and how the next generation of engineers learns.

We're being told that good engineering judgment matters more than ever, while simultaneously removing many of the experiences that traditionally helped us develop it in the first place.

So how do we develop that judgment if we no longer have to go through the experiences that taught us?

I don't really have a conclusion. I don't think anyone does yet, whoever says they know what the future looks like are either lying or don’t know what the heck they are talking about.

These are just some of the things I have been thinking about as the definition of being a software engineer continues to change every week.
